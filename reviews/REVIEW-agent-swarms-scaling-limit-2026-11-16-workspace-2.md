# Swarms scale by cutting conversation, not agents

No measured head-count limit exists for LLM agents. The widely repeated "degrades beyond three to four agents" line comes from Google and MIT. It was not found by adding agents and watching accuracy fall. It is an extrapolation from a power law fitted to coordination turns, inside a study that split one fixed reasoning budget across every agent. That study's own direct sweep peaked at five to seven agents ([Kim et al., arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)). What the controlled evidence does show is narrower and more useful. Agents that converse, share one context, split one budget, or work a task with sequential dependencies get worse as you add more. Agents that each bring their own budget, work an independent shard, pass back only a compact artefact, and feed a verifier or a vote can scale to hundreds. Even then the returns flatten at around 100 ([MacNet, arXiv 2406.07155](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07155)). Every published swarm that produces one result has three things: a source of independent work units, an automatic acceptance check for each unit, and a single point where results merge. On that basis the operator is right about what a swarm needs and too narrow about how it must be built. A planner-decomposed graph is the most general way to get those three things. A strong test oracle can stand in for the planner, as in Anthropic's 16-agent compiler with no orchestrator. Git can stand in for the merge node, as in Cursor's hundreds of agents with no integrator. No surveyed system uses formal contracts between nodes. For a git-backed, record-first substrate, the binding limits are hosted git's write serialisation and the floor's per-commit cost, not the agents. On GitHub's guidance of about six pushes a minute per repository, one shared branch supports a few dozen agents unless hot coordination state leaves git or spreads across refs ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/repository-limits)).

## The three-to-four ceiling is a fixed-budget extrapolation, not a measured sweep

The earlier report in this series said that per-agent capacity "thins beyond three or four agents" and tied that to the operator's wakes failing at four or five loops. Both halves need correcting. Kim et al.'s third version evaluates **260 configurations** across six benchmarks, five architectures and three model families. Almost all of them hold the worker count at **three**. The independent design runs three agents, the centralised design three sub-agents and an orchestrator, and the decentralised design three agents in debate. So the main study varies architecture, model and task, not head count. Every configuration was "matched for total reasoning-token budget (mean 4,800 tokens per trial)", which means each added agent takes a share of a fixed budget ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)). The famous sentence comes from a fit. The authors fitted turn count as **T = 2.72 × (n + 0.5)^1.724 (R² = 0.974)** and extrapolated it to about 69 turns at six agents and 157 at ten. From that they conclude that "beyond 3–4 agents, per-agent reasoning quality degrades sharply under fixed budgets" ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)). The claim is about arithmetic. Coordination messages grow roughly as n^1.7 while the budget stays flat, so the share left for reasoning falls faster than 1/n. No accuracy drop was observed at four agents.

The paper does contain a real agent-count sweep, and it contradicts the paraphrase. At n ∈ {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} on Gemini models, "Gemini-2.0 Flash exhibits a clear optimum at **7 agents**", and centralised Gemini-2.5 Pro shows diminishing returns only **beyond 5** ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)). The retrieved text does not say which benchmark the sweep used or whether the budget was held fixed, so even this optimum is not cleanly comparable. The paper's statistically robust findings concern something else: the fit between architecture and task. Centralised orchestration gained **+80.8%** on decomposable financial analysis. Every multi-agent design lost **39% to 70%** on sequential planning, and every one was negative on SWE-bench Verified. Across all configurations the mean effect was **−0.3%**, with a 95% interval from −58.7% to +77.2%. A capability-saturation effect (p = 0.004) means extra agents hurt once a single agent already scores above about 45%. A tool-count penalty also holds (p = 0.002) ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)). The often-quoted contrast in error amplification, **17.2x** for independent agents against **4.4x** for centralised ones, is descriptive. Its regression main effect is β = 0.014, p = 0.658 ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296)).

For the operator's own loops, the correction matters. Each seat in a star is a separate session with its own budget and context, so Kim's mechanism (a shared pot split thinner) does not apply to four or five concurrent loops. Failures at that count are better explained mechanically: watches not bound, platform loop guards, logins, a starved machine. Those are the categories the `peer-loops` plan's "what woke the seat" line is built to count, so that plan's Phase 2 record is the right instrument, not a cognitive ceiling.

### Other controlled work agrees that edges and budgets drive the effect

The surrounding evidence lines up behind one reading. When budgets are matched, a single agent holds its own. Tran and Kiela matched thinking-token budgets from 100 to 10,000 on multi-hop reasoning. They found that single agents "consistently match or outperform" sequential, subtask-parallel, role-based, debate and ensemble designs: at 1,000 tokens the single agent scored **0.418** and the best multi-agent design **0.388**. They ground this in the Data Processing Inequality: a message passed between agents cannot carry more information about the answer than the full context it was drawn from ([Tran and Kiela, arXiv 2604.02460](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02460v1)). Multi-agent debate "often fail[s] to outperform simple single-agent baselines" while using more compute ([Zhang et al., arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)). A well-prompted single agent matches the best discussion method ([Wang et al., arXiv 2402.18272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18272)).

When agents must coordinate through peer messages over a network, scale is destructive. In AgentsNet, agents talk only to their neighbours on graph problems such as colouring and leader election. Performance "smoothly decreases as the network grows", and "for 100-agent networks, performance drops to near zero across the board" ([AgentsNet, arXiv 2507.08616](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08616)).

When agents do not talk at all and a vote aggregates them, more agents help. Sampling and voting improved steadily up to an ensemble of **40**. An ensembled Llama2-13B reached 59% on GSM8K against 54% for a single Llama2-70B. Adding debate on top made results worse, attributed to "noise" from reading other agents' answers ([Li et al., arXiv 2402.05120](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05120)).

MacNet is the main thousand-agent counterexample, and it works by reversing every condition of Kim's design. Total compute grows with the number of agents. Each agent passes forward only "the final artifact", which turns quadratic token growth into linear. The graphs are sparse and irregular. Even so, performance follows a logistic curve that saturates around **100 agents**, and full-dialogue baselines suffer context explosion past about 30 ([MacNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07155)). The MAST taxonomy of **1,642** failed traces fits the same picture. Its failures fall into system design and specification (about 44% of annotated failures), inter-agent misalignment (about 32%) and verification (about 24%). Raw model incapacity is not where systems break ([Cemri et al., arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657)).

### Production swarms buy parallel compute and wall-clock time

The reconciliation with production swarms is that their gains are not budget-matched, and they do not pretend to be. Anthropic's research system beat single-agent Opus 4 by **90.2%**. The same post reports that "token usage by itself explains **80%** of the variance" and that multi-agent runs use about **15x** the tokens of a chat ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Moonshot's Kimi K2.5 swarm reports its benefit as "Critical Steps", meaning orchestrator overhead plus the slowest sub-agent per stage. It claims a **3x–4.5x** reduction in the critical steps needed to reach a target score and an 80% cut in runtime. It gives no swarm-against-single accuracy figure ([Kimi K2.5 blog](https://www.kimi.com/blog/kimi-k2-5)). A swarm is a throughput and latency instrument that spends more tokens across many separate context windows. It does not make reasoning smarter per token. Read that way, there is no contradiction: the controlled studies hold the budget fixed and ask about intelligence, and the swarms lift the budget and ask about time.

Moonshot's own example is aggregating "300 YouTuber profiles" into a spreadsheet ([Kimi](https://www.kimi.com/blog/kimi-k2-5)).

## The operator is right about the graph's function and wrong about its form

Tested against these systems, the belief has a strong form and a weak form. The strong form says a swarm only works as a planner-decomposed execution graph whose nodes have their own job, contract and handover, merging into one result. Anthropic's C compiler refutes it. Sixteen Claude Opus 4.6 agents produced about **100,000 lines** of Rust that boots Linux 6.9 on three architectures and passes **99%** of most compiler test suites. There was no planner, no orchestrator, and no channel between agents other than git. An agent claimed a task by pushing a file such as `current_tasks/parse_if_statement.txt`, and "git synchronization forces a second agent claiming the same task to choose another one" ([Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). Cursor refutes the "merge into one" half at larger scale. It removed its integrator node because it "created more bottlenecks than it solved". Workers resolve their own conflicts and push to one branch, and a judge only decides whether the next cycle runs ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Its planners do not emit a static graph. They "continuously explore the codebase and create tasks", and each cycle starts fresh.

The weak form holds everywhere: work must arrive as independently verifiable units, and something must bring the results back in. The compiler run shows this most sharply because it shows the failure. Parallelism came from distinct failing tests. When the Linux kernel became "one giant task", all 16 agents fixed the same bug and overwrote each other: "Having 16 agents running didn't help because each was stuck solving the same task." The fix was to make the oracle decompose again. GCC compiled most kernel files and the new compiler compiled a random subset, so each agent hit a different bug, and delta-debugging then separated failing file pairs ([Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). The test suite did the planner's job. When it stopped producing separable units, the swarm collapsed to one effective agent.

Kimi's two named training failures map onto the operator's model precisely. "Serial collapse" is a planner declining to decompose. "Spurious parallelism", a reward hack that spawns sub-agents that never finish, is nodes without a real handover. Moonshot had to pay for both with explicit reward terms ([Kimi](https://www.kimi.com/blog/kimi-k2-5)). Cursor's abandoned flat designs show what happens without a planner and without an oracle. With shared locks, "twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three". With optimistic concurrency, agents became "risk-averse", made small safe changes, and churned. Hierarchy fixed both ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)).

The precise restatement is this. A swarm producing one result needs a generator of independent work units, an automatic acceptance test for each unit, and a merge point. A planner-decomposed graph is the most general way to supply all three. A strong oracle can replace the planner, and git can replace the merge node. Where tasks are sequential or tightly coupled, where there is no oracle, or beyond about 20 agents on code, the operator's form is the only one with evidence behind it: every published coding system above that size has a planner hierarchy. One part of the belief has no practical support at all, and that is the node contract. No surveyed system declares typed inputs and outputs between sibling tasks. What practice mechanises is the dependency edge (Claude Code tasks stay blocked until their prerequisites complete) and the acceptance gate (a `TaskCompleted` hook can refuse completion with exit code 2). The interface between siblings is left to prompts ([Claude Code agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). Cognition names the consequence as its open problem: children "assume shared state that does not exist" and do not surface discoveries that affect their siblings ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). In practice, the contract is the test and the handover is the repository.

Two further regularities hold across every one-result system above about 20 agents. Agent context is disposable: fresh sessions from a `while true` loop, frozen sub-agents, judge-driven restarts. Persistent state lives only in the repository and the task list ([Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents); [Kimi](https://www.kimi.com/blog/kimi-k2-5)). And the vendors are converging on keeping writes single-threaded for production code. Cognition reports that parallel-writer swarms still see little meaningful adoption and that unstructured negotiating swarms are "mostly a distraction". Parallel writers are reserved for greenfield work with an oracle ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)).

## Git serialises at about six pushes a minute, so hot state leaves it

Git gives a shared branch serialised, compare-and-swap updates. A non-fast-forward rejection is the lost race, which is why claiming by commit works at all. The same property puts every writer to one ref in a queue. GitHub's published guidance is **6 pushes per minute per repository**, **1 merged pull request per minute**, **15 git reads per second** and **5,000 branches** before repository health degrades ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/repository-limits)).

The following arithmetic is mine, not measured. If each agent pushes once per completed task and tasks take five to ten minutes, six pushes a minute supports about **30–60 agents** on one hosted repository. The compiler pattern needs at least two pushes per task: one to take the claim, and one to deliver the result and release it. That halves the figure to roughly 15–30, and every lost race adds a fetch, a rebase and another push. On reads, 100 agents each polling the remote every five seconds produce 20 fetches a second, over the guidance.

Hashimoto's view, relayed by a merge-queue vendor, is that serial queues become untenable at agent volumes: "Every time you push it's rejected." The proposed fixes are batching, speculative testing of stacked changes and bisecting failed batches. Those move the binding constraint onto CI cost and flaky tests, and no measured data is offered ([Danjou](https://julien.danjou.info/blog/merge-queues-built-for-humans/)).

Merge conflicts compound the problem. In 33,596 agent-authored pull requests, co-active pairs from different agents conflicted textually **41.7%** of the time, against **19.8%** for pairs from the same agent. About 42% of the conflicts were structural, such as one side deleting a file the other modified, or both adding the same path ([Xu et al., arXiv 2607.04697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04697)).

The published runs show how practice escapes these limits, and the pattern is consistent: hot coordination state leaves git, and accepted results stay in it. Anthropic avoided hosted limits entirely, with one bare repository on local disk mounted into 16 containers ([Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). Cursor gives no hosting or branching detail for its claim of hundreds of agents pushing to one branch "with minimal conflicts" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Gas Town, which targets 20–30 agents, needed a dedicated Refinery agent just to serialise merges ([rywalker.com](https://rywalker.com/research/gastown)). Its git-backed tracker Beads has since moved to Dolt as its only backend and added work leases and an HTTP API ([Thoughtworks Radar](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/radar/tools/beads)). Claude Code keeps its team task list and mailboxes as runtime JSON outside the repository ([Claude Code agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)).

The one measured design that keeps coordination inside git does it with appends. Sarkar's tool stores a signed, append-only event log in git with no server. Every replica converges and no write is silently dropped, while a file-based tracker lost concurrent writes. The tool reports duplicate work falling from **78% to 0%** and useful throughput more than tripling. This is a single-author preprint about the author's own tool ([Sarkar, arXiv 2606.19616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19616)). The principle is sound independently of the tool: one new file or ref per event never collides at the text level, whereas edits to a shared mutable file always eventually do.

## What a record-first substrate needs before it can host a swarm

The operator's substrate already has most of the right parts, aimed at a different scale. Tickets whose status names whose turn it is, a star by default, peers only for bounded tasks, advisory claims with leases, and workflow runs with a floor-checked stage cursor were all designed for a handful of long-lived seats with a human present. The evidence suggests keeping that design for what it does well and adding a separate layer for swarm work. Stretching the conversational parts to cover it would be a mistake.

### Keep tickets for exceptions, not for units of work

The ticket is a conversation carrier, and conversation is the coordination mode that scales worst. Kim's turns grow as n^1.72, AgentsNet's peer network falls to near zero at 100, and a third of MAST failures are inter-agent misalignment ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296); [AgentsNet](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08616); [Cemri et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657)). In a swarm, workers should not converse at all. Cursor's workers "don't coordinate with other workers", and the compiler's agents had no channel besides git ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents); [Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). The star's existing rules are the right ones at swarm scale. A spoke hands its turn only to the hub, and the hub cites a spoke's commit rather than retelling it, which is MacNet's artefact-only passing under another name. Tickets should carry only what rule 13 already describes: a blocked worker addressing the seat that can unblock it.

The unit of work needs its own record, separate from any message: one thing per node, holding its job, its dependency edges and its acceptance check. The framework's existing floor rule, that a terminal thing may not depend on unfinished work, already gives the "must finish first" edge that Claude Code's task list mechanises. A `workflow-run` per node, with stages such as claimed, built, verified and merged and a floor-enforced transition graph, gives the node lifecycle. What is missing is the fan-in. A parent run's merge stage has to be gated on its children reaching a terminal stage, and a planner or an oracle has to generate the children. Neither exists as a primitive today, and both are where the operator's "execution graph" belief should be made concrete.

### Make the acceptance check mechanical and put it in the leaves

Every swarm that worked had an automatic verifier, and verification failures make up about a quarter of MAST's failure modes ([Cemri et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657)). The pre-commit floor is the natural gate. A node's transition to "verified" should be refused unless its declared check passes, which is the equivalent of Claude Code's `TaskCompleted` exit 2 and of each child Devin running its own tests before reporting back ([Claude Code agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams); [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/devin-can-now-manage-devins)). The `peer-loops` plan already requires "a check that decides done" before granting peers a task. That condition is exactly what made the compiler's flat swarm work, and its absence is what made Cursor's flat swarm churn. It should be the gate for any swarm, star or peer.

### Claims must be pushed, cheap and append-only to survive contention

The advisory claim (`held_by`, `held_until`) answers the right question. Its current form, two frontmatter fields edited on the contended thing, is the shared mutable file that Cursor's and Sarkar's data say degrades under load. Three changes would bring it in line with the evidence. First, a claim counts only once it is on the remote ref. This matches the standing watch's rule of reading remote refs, never the working tree, and it makes the push race the compare-and-swap, as in the compiler. Second, a claim should be a new, create-only file per claim and per release, so concurrent claims on different units never conflict and history is a log, not a rewrite. Third, every writer in a swarm must take claims. The current asymmetry, where the scheduled side claims and the operator's session does not, is acceptable for two writers and fatal at twenty, because Cursor lists "edited without locking" among the failures that reduced twenty agents to the throughput of two or three ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). The framework's own lesson from its first deployment, to size the lease against the retry cadence rather than the work, is the direct answer to Cursor's orphaned and over-held locks. It should be carried over unchanged.

### Replace doorbells with a launcher and a queue

The standing watch and its doorbells exist to wake a few long-lived sessions when a turn lands. A swarm does not wait for turns. Its workers are disposable sessions launched against a queue, like the compiler's `while true` loop or Cursor's fresh-start cycles ([Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Arming one watch per worker against a hosted remote would also hit the read guidance. One fetcher per machine, fanning changes out locally, is the minimum. Doorbells remain right for the hub and the human, and Cursor's admission that "planners don't yet wake up when their tasks complete" is precisely a doorbell problem at the hub ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). The division is clean: wakes for seats, a launcher loop for workers.

### Where git, and the floor, become the bottleneck

On a hosted remote, a single shared branch will bind first, somewhere in the tens of agents (by the arithmetic above). The writes per agent are higher in this framework than in the compiler run, because `post-write:commit` makes every state change a commit and taking or releasing a claim is an ordinary write. The remedies, in order of how little they disturb the record-first design, are these. Run a local bare repository for the swarm's working phase, as Anthropic did. Give each worker its own branch or ref and serialise integration through one merge step, which moves contention to the merge queue's one merge a minute. Keep claims and heartbeats in append-only refs or outside git, and commit only accepted results and decisions to the main line ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/repository-limits); [Anthropic compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)).

Before any of those, the floor itself is a candidate bottleneck that no external source measures. The pre-commit hook runs validation and coherence on every commit. The framework's own scalability guide calls 200–300 things comfortable and says the approach should be reconsidered near 1,000. A swarm of 100 workers completing ten units each produces 1,000 node records in a single job. So a swarm should keep its node records in their own corpus, or retire them to stubs on merge, rather than growing the framework corpus. Per-commit floor latency at that scale should be measured before the first real run.

## Conclusion

The headline number was the least reliable thing in the evidence. "Three to four agents" is a statement about a fixed budget split by conversation, and once it is read that way, swarms and the controlled studies stop disagreeing. Swarms work by refusing every condition that produced the ceiling. They do not share a budget, a context or a conversation, and they put a verifier where agents would otherwise negotiate. That reframes the design question for this substrate. The scarce resource in a swarm is coordination edges, both between agents and against the shared ref, not agents themselves. Every primitive should be judged by how many edges it adds per unit of work. Tickets add conversational edges and belong at the hub. Claims add write edges and should be appended, not edited. The acceptance check removes edges, because it lets a unit close without anyone talking about it, and it is the primitive most worth building first.

The operator's graph instinct is the best default for a reason the operator did not state. What the planner contributes is not the graph. It is a reliable stream of separable, checkable units, which is exactly what the compiler's oracle supplied and what failed when the oracle stopped producing them. The most valuable addition to this substrate is therefore probably a mechanised check per node and a fan-in gate on the parent, ahead of any richer contract between nodes. No system in the field has shown that typed sibling contracts pay for themselves, and every system that scaled had the check. A measured run, even at 16 agents on a local bare repository with claim races and floor latency recorded, would be among the very few published data points on how a git-backed swarm actually scales.
