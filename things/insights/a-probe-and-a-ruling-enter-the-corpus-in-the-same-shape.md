# A Probe and a Ruling Enter the Corpus in the Same Shape

## The Insight

In a system whose whole design is that inputs become durable state, **an
experiment has the same shape as a decision.** The operator tries something to
find out whether it works; the corpus receives an act, records it, reasons from
it, and propagates its consequences. Nothing in the shape distinguishes *I am
testing this* from *I have decided this*, because the distinction lives only in
the intent, and intent is exactly what a record cannot infer.

This is a cost of the design, not a user error. The same property that makes
the substrate valuable — it takes you at your word and never quietly forgets —
is what makes an unlabelled probe harden into doctrine.

## The Instance (2026-08-29)

The operator sent a test email through the dispatch host, to find out whether
email worked as an output channel. It did not work — an unconfigured API
refused it. In the reasoning that followed he concluded Notion was the better
channel anyway, because that is where the estate's task lists already live.

By the end of the day that sequence had produced: a channel ruling recorded as
doctrine, a `conflict` in a regulated domain between a live fired trigger
instructing an email and the ruling forbidding one, and an agent (this one)
escalating the original attempt into a governance argument about external-party
effects. His own summary: *"the email decision was just a test. I just wanted
to test it. It wasn't a ruling."*

Not one step was wrong. The trigger genuinely said email. The ruling genuinely
said Notion. The conflict genuinely existed and was correctly surfaced rather
than silently resolved. The cost was small — a minute of the operator's
attention. But the chain ran entirely on an input that was never meant to
start one.

## Why It Matters More Now

Under `settled-reasoning-is-standing-authority` the agent acts where the corpus
already holds evidence and a decided direction. A probe that reads as a
decision therefore no longer waits to be confirmed — it becomes an input the
system may act on without asking. The faster the loop closes, the shorter the
distance between an experiment and a rule derived from it.

## The Remedy, and Why It Is Cheap

**Declare provisionality at the moment of the input, not afterwards.** One
sentence — *"testing whether this works; not a ruling on channels"* — costs
nothing at the time and prevents the entire chain. Retrofitting it costs a
conflict, a correction, and a reconciliation pass.

The agent's half is a calibration, and it is the half that failed here: **not
every observation needs to become a governance question.** An unconfigured API
refusing a test is a fact about configuration. Reading it as a boundary event
and arguing doctrine from it is what turned a probe into a precedent.

## The Shape Worth Keeping

Where every input becomes state, the system cannot distinguish exploration from
commitment unless the input says which. Look for this wherever a corpus is fed
by conversation: the first cost is a spurious rule, and the second — larger —
is the operator learning to be guarded about experimenting at all, which is the
opposite of what a learning substrate is for.
